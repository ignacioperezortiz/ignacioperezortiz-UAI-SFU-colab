\section{Rolling-horizon orchestration and computation time}

The rolling horizon runs through 48 half-hour periods. At each ``now'' period the day is rotated so
that \emph{now} is slot~1 and the \textbf{service block} $\tstar$ is slot~2; a committed dispatch is
solved, it is executed, and 12 directions are swept to trace the region offered at $\tstar$. The
formulation did not change: what changed is \emph{what is solved and how many times}.

Both changes were found by profiling the existing sweep, and both are safe only because of how that
sweep was already built: the 12 directions genuinely share one feasible region, and the un-rotated
inputs are already frozen once per run. What changed is the placement of work that was reasonable
when the sweep was written and no longer pays off in the configuration now used in production, a
continuous QCP solved by barrier. Reviewed and agreed before pushing.

\subsection{The loop, before and after}

\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.475\textwidth}
{\small\textbf{Now} --- 13 solves and 1 load per period}
\vspace{2pt}\hrule height 0.4pt
\vspace{4pt}

for each period $t$:\\

\hspace*{2mm}execute slot 1, advance SOC\\
\hspace*{2mm}for each direction $\theta_k$, $k=1..12$:\\

\hspace*{6mm}solve FOR\_Vertex($\theta_k$)\\
}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{6pt}

From one period to the next \textbf{one single thing} is carried over: the realised state of charge
$e_{b}^{0}$, which advances with the executed dispatch of slot~1 and comes out of the
\emph{baseline}, not out of the sweep. The sweep is handed two: that $e_b^0$ and the dispatch of
slot~1 \emph{fixed}, which guarantees that the offered region is reachable from what the batteries
actually do.

\subsection{Where the time went}

The removed solve, $\min\ \mathrm{OF}^{\text{imp}+\text{rev}}$, ran once per direction.
Its objective \textbf{does not contain the angle}, and within a period the 12 directions share
exactly the same feasible region: same reset, same data rotation, same $e_b^0$ and the same fixed
slot~1. Therefore

\begin{keybox}
The 12 solves of a period were \textbf{the same optimization problem} --- same matrix, same
objective, same solution --- solved 12 times. Its purpose was to seed the vertex solve: the answer
was kept only as the starting point (\emph{warm start}), and nothing else downstream read it.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The interventions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item[\nw{(E)} Splitting the loader.] The reading of the database and Excel was split into a
  \emph{static} half, which runs once per run, and a per-solve \emph{reset} that restores only the
  variables that the emptying destroys: the fixing of $q_{b,t}$, the flat voltage seed and the
  linearization point. Data loads per period: $13 \rightarrow 1$.
  \item[\nw{(A)} Removal of the per-direction warm-start solve.] It was suppressed. Solves per
  period: $25 \rightarrow 13$. It remains behind a switch that restores the previous behaviour, and
  it is kept \emph{active} in the MIQCP validations, where the start channel is real.
  \item[\nw{(C)} Fix in the diagnostic passes.] On splitting the loader, the retries of failed
  directions stopped inheriting the experimental SOC floor; it is re-applied explicitly so that the
  retry runs under the same condition as the direction that failed.
\end{description}

\subsection{Interior point: why the warm start did not help here}

\textbf{What a \emph{warm start} is.} Handing the solver a starting point already close to the
answer, so that less ground is left for it to cover. It is standard practice and it usually helps.

\textbf{Why it did not help here.} It depends on the method, and the two available ones look for the
optimum in opposite ways:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.20\textwidth} p{0.36\textwidth} p{0.36\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Simplex} & \textbf{Barrier (interior point)}\\
\midrule
Where it advances & along the \textbf{edge}, jumping from vertex to vertex & through the
\textbf{interior}, following the central path\\
\addlinespace[2pt]
Ideal starting point & a vertex close to the optimum --- a basis & a \textbf{well-centred} point,
far from every active constraint\\
\addlinespace[2pt]
Effect of starting on the boundary & it brings it closer: few jumps are missing & it
\textbf{penalises} it: it must first re-centre\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The point that the code handed over was the optimum of \emph{another} problem, the minimum-cost one.
And the optimum of a convex program with a linear objective is, by construction, an \textbf{extreme
point}: stuck against the boundary, with several active constraints. It is exactly what an
interior-point method does not want --- hence the name of the method: there is a barrier term that
pushes it \emph{away} from the constraints, and starting on one of them forces that work to be
undone before it can advance. In the corridor image: barrier walks down the middle, and it was being
handed the point flattened against the wall.

\textbf{The change.} Suppress that solve. \emph{No equation was touched.} The vertex solve now
starts from the flat point that the reset leaves --- all voltages at their balanced nominal value
---, comfortably interior and with no active constraints, which is just what barrier needs.

\textbf{Measured result:} $1.27$ s $\rightarrow$ $0.62$ s per vertex solve. \emph{Half, from no
longer helping it.}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{An honest nuance about the mechanism.} What is verified is the \emph{measurement}:
2.05$\times$ per solve, with bit-identical output. The explanation of the badly centred point is the
best supported one, but it has a known tension: on the continuous path the solver does not receive a
basis --- that channel exists only on the integer path --- and the first solve, also a barrier one,
does not produce one to hand over either. That the difference is measured all the same indicates
that something of the previous state has an influence through a route that was not identified. The
\emph{what} is proven; the \emph{why} is a supported hypothesis, not a verified fact.
\end{keybox}

Note that, even accepting the warm-start theory, \textbf{11 of the 12 solves were redundant
anyway}: it was enough to solve it once per period and reuse the point. Both programs declare the
same sets of constraints and variables, so that they generate a row-for-row identical matrix and
differ only in the objective column --- which is why removing one does not alter the other.

Numerical options kept: feasibility tolerance $10^{-3}$, barrier convergence tolerance
$10^{-2}$, aggressive scaling, and the compact generator of quadratic programs --- needed because
the inherited generator expanded the quadratics and exhausted the memory towards period
37 of 48.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Equivalence verified.} $\max|\Delta\,\mathrm{proj}| = 0.000\mathrm{e}{+}00$ in all four

$\theta^\top(P,Q)$, not the equality of $(P,Q)$: a flat face perpendicular to the sweep direction
turns the argument of the maximum into a segment, and the returned point may legitimately slide along it.
\end{keybox}

\vspace{2pt}
The real bottleneck was not the data reading but the \textbf{generation of the mathematical
program}: on TR9 each generation of a QCP with 90 batteries and 341 buses costs of the order of
30\,s, and \nw{(A)} removes 12 generations per period on top of 12 solves.

\section{Formulation: multiple aggregators and fairness at two levels}

Unbalanced three-phase current-injection model, with linearization of the load and generation
injections around a point $(\vre_0,\vim_0)$ and exact quadratic constraints for maximum voltage and
inverter capacity. Everything in \nw{red} is new.

\subsection{Sets and indices}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{\hspace{2.2em}} l l@{}}
$\Nset$ & buses $n$ & $\Bset$ & batteries / prosumers $b$\\
$\Lset$ & lines $\ell$ & $\Fset=\{1,2,3\}$ & phases $f,h$\\
$\Tset$ & transformers $k$ & $\Hset=\{1,\dots,48\}$ & periods $t$\\
$\Dset$ & loads with PV $d$ & \nw{$\Aset$} & \nw{aggregators $\alpha$}\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\tstar$ denotes the service block; in the rolling horizon $\tstar = 2$ after the rotation.
\nw{$a(b)\in\Aset$} is \nw{the aggregator of prosumer $b$}, an input datum.

\subsection{Objective function of the directional sweep}

For each direction $\theta_k = 30^\circ(k-1)$, $k=1,\dots,12$:

\begin{equation}
\min\ \ -\bigl(\cos\theta_k\, P_{\tstar} + \sin\theta_k\, Q_{\tstar}\bigr)
\end{equation}

with the power at the connection point, positive from grid towards feeder,

\begin{align}
P_t &= \sum_{g}\ \vre_{\mathrm{pcc},f(g)}\, \ire_{g,t} + \vim_{\mathrm{pcc},f(g)}\, \iim_{g,t}\\
Q_t &= \sum_{g}\ \vim_{\mathrm{pcc},f(g)}\, \ire_{g,t} - \vre_{\mathrm{pcc},f(g)}\, \iim_{g,t}
\end{align}

Minimising $-\theta_k^\top(P,Q)$ evaluates the \emph{support function} of the feasible set in the
direction $\theta_k$; by rotating $\theta$ the boundary of the region is traced.

\subsection{Network}

The voltage is anchored at the reference bus $r$, fixed and balanced, and it is what sets the origin
of the whole system:
\begin{equation}
\vre_{r,f,t} = \begin{cases}1 & f=1\\ -\tfrac12 & f\in\{2,3\}\end{cases}
\qquad
\vim_{r,f,t} = \begin{cases}0 & f=1\\ -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & f=2\\ +\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & f=3\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Current balance at each bus and phase:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{g\in n,f}\!\ire_{g,t} + \!\!\sum_{\ell:\,\mathrm{to}(\ell)=n}\!\!\ire_{\ell,f,t}
+ \!\!\sum_{k:\,\mathrm{to}(k)=n}\!\!\ire_{k,f,n,t}
- \!\!\sum_{\ell:\,\mathrm{from}(\ell)=n}\!\!\ire_{\ell,f,t}
- \!\!\sum_{k:\,\mathrm{from}(k)=n}\!\!\ire_{k,f,n,t}
= \!\!\sum_{d\in n,f}\!\!\bigl(\ireL_{d,t}-\ireG_{d,t}\bigr) + \!\!\sum_{b\in n,f}\!\!\ire_{b,t}
\end{equation}
and its analogue in the imaginary part. Voltage drop on lines, with mutual coupling:
\begin{align}
\vre_{\mathrm{from}(\ell),f,t}-\vre_{\mathrm{to}(\ell),f,t}
&= \sum_{h\neq f}\!\bigl(R^{m}_{\ell}\ire_{\ell,h,t}-X^{m}_{\ell}\iim_{\ell,h,t}\bigr)
 + R^{s}_{\ell}\ire_{\ell,f,t}-X^{s}_{\ell}\iim_{\ell,f,t}\\
\vim_{\mathrm{from}(\ell),f,t}-\vim_{\mathrm{to}(\ell),f,t}
&= \sum_{h\neq f}\!\bigl(X^{m}_{\ell}\ire_{\ell,h,t}+R^{m}_{\ell}\iim_{\ell,h,t}\bigr)
 + X^{s}_{\ell}\ire_{\ell,f,t}+R^{s}_{\ell}\iim_{\ell,f,t}
\end{align}

Dy transformers with tap ratio $\tau_{k,t}$, coupling phase $f$ with the next two:
\begin{equation}
\vre_{\mathrm{from}(k),f,t} = -\tfrac{\nu_k \tau_{k,t}}{3}\Bigl(2\vre_{\mathrm{to}(k),f\oplus1,t}+\vre_{\mathrm{to}(k),f\oplus2,t}
+ R_k\bigl(2\ire_{k,f\oplus1,\cdot,t}+\ire_{k,f\oplus2,\cdot,t}\bigr) - X_k\bigl(2\iim_{k,f\oplus1,\cdot,t}+\iim_{k,f\oplus2,\cdot,t}\bigr)\Bigr)
\end{equation}
The $\vim$ equation is the same with $R_k$ acting on the imaginary currents and $+X_k$ on the real
ones. The primary--secondary current relation is
$\ire_{k,f,\mathrm{from},t}\,\nu_k\tau_{k,t} = \ire_{k,f,\mathrm{to},t}-\ire_{k,f\oplus1,\mathrm{to},t}$,
and analogous in $\iim$. In transformers without on-load tap changing the tap is fixed,
$\tau_{k,t}=\tau_k^{\mathrm{fix}}$, so that the product $\tau_{k,t}\,v$ introduces no
nonlinearity: the model remains affine in the injections.

Voltage limits --- upper exact, lower on the first-order approximation
$\widehat{V^2}$ --- and thermal limits:
\begin{align}
(\vre_{n,f,t})^2+(\vim_{n,f,t})^2 &\le \overline{V}_n^{\,2},
&\widehat{V^2}_{n,f,t} &\ge \underline{V}_n^{\,2}\\
(\ire_{\ell,f,t})^2+(\iim_{\ell,f,t})^2 &\le \overline{I}_\ell^{\,2},
&(\ire_{k,f,\mathrm{from},t})^2+(\iim_{k,f,\mathrm{from},t})^2 &\le \overline{I}_k^{\,2}
\end{align}
where $\widehat{V^2}_{n,f,t} = (\vre_0)^2+(\vim_0)^2+2\vre_0(\vre-\vre_0)+2\vim_0(\vim-\vim_0)$.
The load and PV injections are linearized in the same way, around $(\vre_0,\vim_0)$:
\begin{equation}
\ireL_{d,t} = \frac{P^0_{d,t}\vre_0 + Q^0_{d,t}\vim_0}{(\vre_0)^2+(\vim_0)^2}
+ \frac{\partial \ireL}{\partial \vre}\bigl(\vre-\vre_0\bigr)
+ \frac{\partial \ireL}{\partial \vim}\bigl(\vim-\vim_0\bigr)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Batteries}

With $p_{b,t}$ the net active power of prosumer $b$ ($p_{b,t}>0$ charging, $p_{b,t}<0$ discharging)
and $q_{b,t}$ the reactive power of the inverter:
\begin{align}
p_{b,t} &= \pch_{b,t}-\pdis_{b,t}, &\pch_{b,t},\ \pdis_{b,t} &\in[0,\overline{P}_b]\\
p_{b,t} &= \vre_{0,b,t}\,\ire_{b,t}+\vim_{0,b,t}\,\iim_{b,t},
&q_{b,t} &= -\vre_{0,b,t}\,\iim_{b,t}+\vim_{0,b,t}\,\ire_{b,t}\\
e_{b,t} &= e^0_b + \frac{\Delta t}{E_b}\sum_{\tau\le t}\Bigl(\eta^{c}_b \pch_{b,\tau}-\frac{\pdis_{b,\tau}}{\eta^{d}_b}\Bigr),
&\underline{\sigma}_b &\le e_{b,t}\le \overline{\sigma}_b
\end{align}
where $e_{b,t}$ is the state of charge as a fraction and $\underline{\sigma}_b,\overline{\sigma}_b$
its bounds --- distinct from the capacity $E_b$, which appears in the denominator and is in units of energy.
Inverter capacity at the service block (exact quadratic cone) and cyclic return of the
state of charge over the window:
\begin{equation}
p_{b,\tstar}^{2}+q_{b,\tstar}^{2}\ \le\ \overline{P}_b^{\,2},
\qquad
\Bigl|\sum_{t\in\Hset}\Bigl(\eta^{c}_b \pch_{b,t}-\frac{\pdis_{b,t}}{\eta^{d}_b}\Bigr)\Bigr| \le \beta E_b/\Delta t
\end{equation}
Reactive power is released only at $\tstar$: $q_{b,t}=0$ for $t\neq\tstar$. In the sweep, the
dispatch of slot~1 is fixed to the committed value: $\pch_{b,1}=\hat{p}^{c}_b$,
$\pdis_{b,1}=\hat{p}^{d}_b$. The model also keeps the inherited constraint
$p_{b,\tstar}\le \kappa\,\overline{P}_b$, where $\kappa=0$ reproduces the assumption of the paper
--- batteries only discharging at the service block --- and $\kappa=1$ is the production
configuration, bidirectional; with $\kappa=1$ the constraint is dominated by
$\pch_{b,\tstar}\le\overline{P}_b$ and is not binding.

\subsection{\nw{Fairness: proportional criterion C1}}

\begin{nuevo}
\textbf{\nw{New.}} Let \nw{$s_b$} be the nominal capacity of prosumer $b$ --- \nw{$s_b=\overline{P}_b$}
is adopted, which in this model is at once the power of the battery and the radius of the
circle of inverter capacity. Let \nw{$\zeta\in\mathbb{R}$} be a \textbf{single free scalar per
solve}, interpretable as the \emph{utilisation factor} of the fleet. With
\nw{$m\in\{0,1,2\}$} the fairness level and \nw{$\chi\in\{0,1\}$} the activation gate:

\vspace{-3mm}
\begin{align}
\nw{p_{b,\tstar}} &\ \nw{=\ \zeta\, s_b} &&\nw{\forall b\in\Bset,}
&&\nw{\text{if } m=1 \ \text{and}\ \chi=1} \label{eq:c1p}\\[2pt]
\nw{\sum_{b:\,a(b)=\alpha} p_{b,\tstar}} &\ \nw{=\ \zeta \!\!\sum_{b:\,a(b)=\alpha}\!\! s_b}
&&\nw{\forall \alpha\in\Aset,} &&\nw{\text{if } m=2 \ \text{and}\ \chi=1} \label{eq:c1a}
\end{align}
\end{nuevo}

\textbf{A single degree of freedom.} The criterion is written with \emph{one} scalar $\zeta$, which
removes \emph{one} degree of freedom. Writing it as $n-1$ pairwise equalities would remove $n-1$ and
would overestimate the cost of fairness by a factor close to two.

\textbf{Scope of each level.} Level~2 constrains only the sums per aggregator; the internal split
of each aggregator remains free. The levels are alternative, not cumulative.

\textbf{Consequence of the sign.} Since $p_{b,\tstar}=\pch-\pdis$, a single $\zeta$ forces the whole
fleet to the same sign: \emph{it forbids one battery to charge while another discharges}. It is the
strictest reading of the criterion, and that is why C1 gives the \textbf{upper bound} on the cost of fairness.

\textbf{Feasibility.} $\zeta=0$ is always admissible, so that C1 \emph{contracts} the region and
never makes it infeasible. With $s_b=\overline{P}_b$ the implied bound is $|\zeta|\le1$.

\subsection{\nw{The gate $\chi$}}

$\chi$ takes the value $1$ in the 12 directional solves and $0$ in the solve of the committed
dispatch. The reason is not one of implementation:

\begin{keybox}
$\tstar$ is slot~2 \emph{also} in the baseline, so that without the gate
\eqref{eq:c1p}--\eqref{eq:c1a} would constrain the dispatch executed in slot~1, the trajectory of
the state of charge would diverge from the case without fairness, and the comparison would measure
two mixed effects: the contraction of the region, and a different battery state. Moreover the
objective of the baseline contains no service term at $\tstar$ --- by construction $w_2(\tstar)=0$
--- so imposing a market rule there would constrain an agent that obtains no value at all from
participating in it.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Cases and nesting}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{c l l}
\toprule
$m$ & Case & Constraint active at $\tstar$\\
\midrule
$0$ & No fairness (reference) & --- no row generated\\
$1$ & \nw{Proportional between prosumers} & \nw{\eqref{eq:c1p}}\\
$2$ & \nw{Proportional between aggregators} & \nw{\eqref{eq:c1a}}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

With $m=0$ both domains are empty: no row is generated and $\zeta$ remains as a free column with no
coefficients, which presolve removes. That is the reason why the reference case reproduces the
previous model exactly.

\textbf{Nesting.} Level~1 implies level~2: by summing \eqref{eq:c1p} over the prosumers of
an aggregator, \eqref{eq:c1a} is obtained with the same $\zeta$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}(m{=}1)\ \subseteq\ \mathcal{X}(m{=}2)\ \subseteq\ \mathcal{X}(m{=}0)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{proj}_{\theta}^{(1)}\ \le\ \mathrm{proj}_{\theta}^{(2)}\ \le\ \mathrm{proj}_{\theta}^{(0)}
\ \ \forall\theta,\ \forall t
\end{equation}
which is the acceptance test: a constraint cannot enlarge the feasible set, and any
violation is a defect and not a result.

\subsection{Results on the micro network}

Full verification on the 576 vertices: the reference case reproduces the previous model with
exact $\Delta=0$ and byte-identical state of charge; the nesting holds without exception; and the
constraint is satisfied with a maximum residual of $10^{-4}$, an order stricter than the solver's own
feasibility tolerance.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Two observations.} \emph{(i)} The loss of \emph{area} is much larger than that of projection
per direction, whose median is practically nil: where the boundary has a flat face
perpendicular to the sweep direction, the optimum slides to a fair point at no cost in that
direction. Both metrics must be reported. \emph{(ii)} Scaling a two-dimensional region by $\gamma$
scales its area by $\gamma^2$: an area loss of 87\,\% is equivalent to a linear shrinkage to
$\sim$36\,\%, not to 13\,\%.
\end{keybox}

The fleet is homogeneous --- $\overline{P}_b = 3.6$ kW in the 1,216 batteries --- so that on this
data the proportional criterion coincides numerically with the egalitarian one. That does not make
it innocuous: the network is radial, and two prosumers with the same battery at different electrical
positions cannot inject the same amount without producing different voltage rises. The
heterogeneity that makes C1 bind is \textbf{locational}, not equipment.
